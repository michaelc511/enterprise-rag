S1:1 Data Sources: The Business, the Company, and the Documents

The Business: Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM)

Before a company lets a vendor touch its data, procurement, security, and GRC (governance, risk & compliance) teams read the vendor's contract and its SOC 2 audit report and check both against the company's own security policy. It's slow, manual, and high-stakes: one missed clause can mean customer data used to train someone else's AI model, or a breach the company hears about days too late. This product is a RAG assistant that does that review with cited answers, in two directions:

  • Inbound (vendor review): "Is this vendor safe?" Check a vendor's MSA and SOC 2 report against our policy and flag gaps.
  • Outbound (security questionnaires): "Prove we're safe." Answer a customer's security questionnaire with citations from our own policies.

It's used by procurement, security, GRC, legal, and sales teams. Not legal advice.

The Company: OpsVault (fictional)

What OpsVault does: it's a cloud enterprise workflow platform: one platform that runs a company's IT, HR, customer service, risk, finance, and supply-chain work, with AI agents built in. Customers buy ready-made products (SaaS) or build their own apps on the OpsVault Platform (PaaS).

What OpsVault sells:

AreaProducts
Platform & AIOpsVault Platform (low-code, workflow engine, APIs), OpsVault AI (assistants and agents), App Builder
IT & SecurityIT Service (incidents, changes, requests), IT Operations (CMDB, monitoring, AIOps), Security Ops
RiskRisk & Compliance (policies, audits, vendor risk): this assistant's home
People & CustomersHR Service, Customer Service, CRM
Finance & Supply ChainFinance Ops (invoices, close, spend), Supply Chain (supplier onboarding, source-to-pay): this assistant's second home, ERP Connect (SAP, Oracle, NetSuite sync)

How OpsVault works: it follows a Customer Zero approach. Its own teams use a product first, then it's sold to customers. This assistant follows the same path: OpsVault's GRC team reviews its own vendors with it first, then it ships as an OpsVault AI skill inside Risk & Compliance.

Compliance: OpsVault holds a SOC 2 Type II report (Security, Availability, Confidentiality). SOX applies to finance vendors and PCI DSS wherever card data is involved. It does not hold HIPAA attestation or ISO 27001.

  • One data folder per use case. A future use case gets data/<its-name>/ and never mixes with these documents or this index.
  • The folder name is a label. S4 can tag each chunk's document type from its folder (msa, sow, …).
  • Originals are never changed. Later steps redact and label chunks in memory; raw/ stays the untouched record.
  • Documents stay out of git. data/ is gitignored, so company documents never reach the repo.

 46  ## PII protection (three layers)
 47  **Redact people, not companies.**
 48  - **Personal data of any individual** (customer, employee, signatory, auditor, notice contact): names,
 49    signatures, emails, phone numbers, home addresses, government IDs.
 50  - **Secrets and financial data:** bank account and routing numbers, payment card numbers, API keys,
 51    passwords.
 52  - **Keep:** vendor, company, and contract-party names, business addresses, and contract terms, so
 53    metadata (S4) and questions like "the cloud vendor's MSA" still work.
 54  
 55  **How:**
 56  - **Typed placeholders, not blanks:** `[PERSON_1]`, `[EMAIL_2]`, `[BANK_ACCOUNT_1]`, numbered per
 57    document so answers stay readable ("signed by [PERSON_1]").
 58  - **Detection:** Microsoft Presidio (NER) for names, locations, and IDs, plus regex for card numbers,
 59    bank numbers, and keys.
 60  - **Redaction log:** per chunk, record the entity type and count, never the original value. This is the
 61    audit trail.
 62  
 63  **Layers:**
 64  1. **Ingestion (S5):** redact before embedding or storage.
 65  2. **Query (S8):** redact the user's question before it reaches the LLM.
 66  3. **Answer (S12):** check the final answer before returning it.

S1:7 Document-by-Document Walkthrough: How the Corpus Fits Together

  • Start with the policy. POL-SEC-007 is the yardstick. It defines a Critical Vendor (anyone touching Customer Data) and the eight vendor rules: SOC 2 Type II (VM-1), AES-256 at rest with yearly key rotation (VM-2), TLS 1.2+ (VM-3), 48-hour incident notice (VM-4), no AI training on customer data even if de-identified (VM-5), 30-day deletion (VM-6), a 2x-fees breach cap (VM-7), and a yearly audit right (VM-8).
  • The framework connects policy to SOC 2. POL-GRC-002 maps criteria like CC6.1 and CC7.4 to OpsVault controls, and lists the customer-facing commitments that outbound answers may use.
  • Stratocore is the "mostly good" vendor. Its MSA meets the policy on paper. Its NDA came first and is cited by the MSA. Its two SOWs both point back to MSA-2025-014. Its SOC 2 Type II is clean except for one key-rotation exception.
  • Cortexa is the "risky" vendor. Its MSA looks fine at §5.3 ("no model training") but §5.4 allows training on de-identified usage data. Its notice window, liability cap, and audit rights all fall short, and it only has a Type I report.
  • Harborline is a different kind of NDA. It adds non-solicitation and a rule for shipment personal data, so not every NDA question has the same answer.
  • The questionnaire flips the direction. Brightwater asks OpsVault to prove its own controls, including two questions (HIPAA, ISO 27001) whose honest answer is "No".

Personal data and secrets in the documents (for S5)

Signers' and contacts' names, work emails, phone numbers, bank routing and account numbers, a leaked API key in SOW-02, a test card number, and auditor and interviewee names. All emails use reserved .example domains and all phone numbers use the 555 range. Company names and business addresses stay, because the redaction rule is "people, not companies".

What it doesn't cover

  • Clean layouts. Real contracts have scanned pages, long definitions sections, and broken layouts. These PDFs are clean, so they don't stress S2 the way real files would. Adding CUAD contracts later fixes this.
  • No labels yet. The gap table is an answer key for a handful of checks, not a labeled dataset; recall@k needs a proper question set.
  • Tables flatten. When extracted, a table comes out as one cell per line. That's realistic, and S2/S3 must handle it.
  • Footer noise. Every page carries a "synthetic document" footer; S2's cleaning should strip it.

S1:9 System Design ↑ S1

Talking points for a system-design discussion.

  • Data: "What datasets would you use?" A curated synthetic corpus first, so every demo question and gap has a known answer; CUAD's lawyer-labeled contracts next, for realism and recall metrics.
  • Governance: the originals are never modified; every page says it's synthetic; company documents never enter git.
  • Systems design: one folder per use case means the pipeline is business-agnostic. A new domain is a new folder and use-case file, not a code change.
  • Evals: the 8 gaps give S13 something concrete to check before any labeled dataset exists.

Trade-offs:

DecisionProsConsAlternative / when to switch
Synthetic documentsEvery clause, cross-reference, gap, and PII item is known; no licensingToo clean; weaker "what dataset?" answerAdd CUAD contracts before building recall metrics
Ignore all of data/Real company documents can never leak into gitDocuments must be shared outside the repoKeep for real documents; a samples/ folder could hold public test files
Fictional company and vendorsNo real company's documents imitatedLess recognizable in a demoKeep fictional; describe the real company type in the pitch
One folder per document typeDocument type comes free for S4 metadataA document of two types needs a choiceMove type into a metadata file if folders stop fitting
